# The Renaissance of Sofware Development

If you are at all active in developer twitter there has been a lot of anxiety going around about the future of software development, AI, and what that means for our jobs.

AI writes a lot of code now. MCP servers and tool calls make it passable. Ralph scaffolds entire apps while we sleep. You can describe an idea, step away for a coffee, and when you return to your screen there is something tangible that actually works. If you spend enough time reading comments online, it can start to feel like the craft is disappearing.

I reject this.

I honestly think there has never been a better time to ship software.

For a long time we were stuck in a rut of mediocrity and framework spaghetti. Our jobs were detangling data pipelines, maintaining legacy code, or worse, migrating between breaking versions of whatever JavaScript framework was popular that year. It was not fun. It was not inspiring. It was not rewarding. It was busy work and complexity of our own making.

Now we can pawn the boring parts off to AI. That frees us to be creative, to think outside the box, to ship things that bring joy. The hardest part of the job now is not writing code. It is having agency. We actually have to think.

The way we write software is constantly changing, and it is genuinely hard to keep up. Every week there is a new idea, model, or piece of software that you are supposed to drop everything for because it is the greatest thing that has ever existed. RAG. Fine tuning. Tools. Skills. MCPs. Agents. The vocabulary never settles.

We have gone from entrenched IDEs to AI first editors. From bespoke templates to generating an idea from a prompt. All encompassing full-stack frameworks to lightweight web frameworks. We have vibe coding, CLIs, AI pair programming, and background agents that write both the code and the documentation.

The tooling changes almost as fast as the software itself. Replit, Lovable, Claude Code, OpenCode, Windsurf, Cursor. Many of these companies barely existed a few years ago and now sit at hundreds of billions in valuation. 

The same is true at the models too. Opus, Codex, Kimi, Grok. The models keep leapfrogging each other. They keep getting better. They keep pushing the ceiling higher.

All of this is to say that the current state, today, is the worst it will ever be. It will keep evolving, and it will evolve fast. These tools are changing how we write code and how we ship software, but they do not remove the human aspect. At least not yet, and I genuinely doubt they ever will.

What they do change is the pace.

Ideas move faster now and experimenting is cheaper than ever. Shipping is no longer a once a sprint event (gross). It is something you can do in an afternoon. That shift changes what we should want from the rest of the stack.

When creation speeds up, everything around it needs to keep up. What you write locally using your CLI-based agent should be able to exist in the real world without friction. Otherwise, why bother building something playful or ambitious if you cannot share it?

You can't keep using platforms that ask you to slow down or force you to context switch just to ship. You want something that keeps up. Something that lets you stay in flow while you are building, then deploys your code so you can put something real in front of people.

Ideally, it is a cloud platform that AI already understands and can work with directly. Something that you can deploy to with a simple confirmation of your AI agent, and not through a legacy dashboard.

What excites me most about this moment is not any single tool or model.

It is that software feels creative again. It feels free, and boundless.

The boring parts are rapidly fading into the background. Building is about taste. You can move fast, try something weird, throw it away, and then try it again. Shipping no longer feels like drudgery, instead it feels like a stream of thought.

And on that note I think we are just getting started.

The platforms that understand this moment, the ones that lean into speed, agency, and simplicity instead of fighting them, are going to define what the next decade of software looks like.

That is a future worth being excited about.
